greater sales and more efficient use of assets

The ROCE comprises two elements, profitability (operating margin) and efficiency
(asset turnover) The board’s plan was to sacrifice profitability in order to stimulate
efficiency, the intention being to increase efficiency to such a degree that overall ROCE
would be improved.

The anticipated effects occurred, but unfortunately the increase in sales of 150%
($120m to $300m) and the related net asset turnover increase from 1.9 to 4.5 times,
was insufficient to compensate for an operating margin that declined to only 0.7% The
board appear to have been overenthusiastic in their price-cutting strategy, a strategy
that is not sustainable in the long term Although they may seek alternative remedies,
such as cost-cutting or negotiating bulk discount with suppliers, the board need to re-

Financial performance
An important feature of Greenwood’s performance in the year to 31 March 20X7 is the
impact of the discontinued operation. When using an entity’s recent results as a basis for
assessing how the entity may perform in the future, emphasis should be placed on the results
from continuing operations as it is these that will form the basis of future results. For this
reason most of the ratios calculated are based on the results from continuing operations, and
ratio calculations involving net assets/capital employed generally exclude the assets held for
sale.
On this basis, it can be seen that the overall performance of Greenwood, measured by its
Return on Capital Employed (ROCE), has declined considerably from 33.5% to 30.9%.  The
ROCE calculation is a combination of efficiency, measured by asset turnover, and profitability,
measured by operating profit margin.

Operating profitability has increased from 17.7% to 18.5%, indicating that the fall in ROCE is
due to efficiency.  The fall in the asset turnover from 1.9 to 1. 7 times confirms that assets
are being used less efficiently to generate sales.  Despite an impressive increase in sales

operations means that it is not possible to identify the cause of this rise in capital employed.
Such a significant increase in revenue is likely to have come from new product, new market
or new customer, all of which would require substantial investment.  It is to be hoped that
the balance between capital employed and revenue will be recovered in the future, as the
impact of the investment grows.
Further profitability analysis shows that Greenwood has maintained its gross profit margin
for its continuing operations at around 29%, possibly aided by bulk purchase discounts.  This
shows that the increase in revenue has not been achieved at the expense of margin.  A
modest improvement in operating margin is due to a reduction in operating expenses.  With
a 30% increase in revenue an increase in operating expenses is to be expected in absolute
terms, and indeed expenses have increased by 18%, indicating that there is probably a fixed
element within the operating expenses.  There may also have been one-off costs connected
with the increase level of sales activity, such as marketing costs.  As these one-off costs will
not be repeated, it is likely that the future operating margin may improve still further.
The decision to sell the activities classified as a discontinued operation is likely to improve
the future profitability of Greenwood. In the year ended 31 March 20X6 the discontinued
operation made a modest pre-tax profit of $450,000 (this would represent a return of around
7% on the activity’s assets of $6.3 million).  This poor return acted to reduce the overall

discontinued operation continued to deteriorate in the year ended 31 March 20X7 making a
pre-tax operating loss of $1.4 million which creates a negative return on the relevant assets.
Despite incurring losses on the measurement to fair value of the discontinued operation’s

operation showed no sign of recovery.

Greenwood’s inventory days have increased significantly.  Given that margins have been
maintained, this is a cause for concern.  The increased levels of inventory may have been
necessary to cope with additional sales, and the inventory turnover may improve once the
sales level has stabilised.
Gearing
The additional borrowing of $3 million in loan notes (perhaps due to liquidity pressure) has
resulted in an increase in gearing from 28.6% to 35.6% and a consequent increase in finance
costs.  Despite the increase in finance costs the borrowing is acting in the shareholders’
favour as the overall return on capital employed at 30.9% is well in excess of the 5% coupon
rate of the loan note.
It is difficult to see what the loan has been used for, given the aggregated nature of 20X6’s
statement of financial position.  On the face of it, non-current assets have hardly moved, but
20X6’s figure includes the discontinued operation, so there could have been significant
investment in this area.  It is to be hoped that the loan has been used wisely.  If it has been
used simply to fund working capital, this would be neither appropriate nor sustainable.
Summary
Overall Greenwood’s performance has deteriorated in the year ended 31 March 20X7, mainly
as a result of declining asset turnover.  Management’s action in respect of the discontinued
operation is a welcome measure to try to halt the decline, but more needs to be done. The
liquidity position is giving cause for serious concern and without the prospect of realising $6
million from the assets held for sale it would be difficult to envisage any easing of
Greenwood’s liquidity pressures.  That said, if the assets held for sale result in the injection
of $6m cash, and Greenwood can return to 20X6’s levels of asset and inventory turnover,
future years may see significant improvement.

(c)
Limitations in the use of ratio analysis

Ratios  are  a  tool  to  assist  analysis.    As  such,  they  help  to  focus  attention
systematically on important areas and summarise information in an understandable
form and assist in identifying trends and relationships.

However ratios cannot be predictive if they are based on historical information and
ignore any future action by management.  Asset values shown in the statement of
financial position at historical cost may bear no resemblance to their current value or
what it may cost to replace them. This may result in a low depreciation charge and
overstatement  of  profit  in  real  terms.  As  a  result  of  historical  costs  the  financial
statements do not show the real cost of using the non‐current assets.  Ratios based
on historical cost accounts do not give a true picture of trends from year to year. An
apparent increase in profit may not be a true increase, and may simply reflect the
effects of inflation.

They  can  be  manipulated  by  window  dressing  or  creative  accounting.    Window
dressing is a method of carrying out transactions in order to improve the position
shown  by  the  financial  statements  at  the  year‐end.    This  could  include  deferring
payment  to  suppliers  in  order  to  enhance  the  cash  position.    Creative  accounting
refers  to  the  accounting  practices  that  are  designed  to  mislead  the  user’s  view  of
underlying economic performance. Typically creative accounting is used to increase
profits,  inflate  asset  values  or  understate  liabilities.    In  the  past  companies  could
smooth  profits  to  maintain  a  steady  upward  trend  by  making  use  of  general
provisions, but this is no longer allowed under IAS 37.

They  may  be  distorted  by  differences  in  accounting  policies.    Choice  of  different
accounting policies may make comparison between entities difficult, for example if
one uses the cost model and another the revaluation model for property.

The  statement  of  financial  position  may  not  be  representative  of  the  financial
position throughout the year.  For example a large purchase of non‐current assets
just before the year‐end, funded by debt finance, would have a significant impact on
ratios such as asset turnover, return on capital employed and gearing, and not show
an  accurate  representation  of  the  performance  during  the  year.    This  can  be
exacerbated by seasonal trading. For example, if an entity has their year‐end after a
particularly busy period then working capital levels would be lower, and may make
the company appear more solvent than during the busy period.
